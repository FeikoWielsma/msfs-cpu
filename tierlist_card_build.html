What to buy at three budgets, for each resolution — with the measured Performance Index on every chip so you can see what the extra money actually buys. GPU picks are matched to the resolution they sit under, and 16 GB is the floor everywhere except the 1080p entry build, where 8 GB is the mildest it ever gets. At what memory now costs, DDR4 platforms are the value play — AM4 and LGA1700 carry every entry build, and on the Intel side the mid builds too.

Reading the matrix

Memory is the story in 2026, and it decides the entry platform. 32 GB of DDR5 is €400 against €240 for DDR4 — so entry stays on AM4 and LGA1700 deliberately. Nobody puts €400 of memory in an €1,100 build. Mid and high move to DDR5 where the platform earns it. Nothing takes 64 GB: that kit now costs more than a 9800X3D.

Radeon is better value at every tier right now. A 9070 XT at €669 is both cheaper and faster than a 5070 Ti at €949 (81 against 70 at 1440p), which is why the XT tops the 1440p and 4K blocks while the Nvidia column spends more for less. The 9070 GRE at €555 is squeezed out entirely: a plain RX 9070 is €34 more for +20%, so there is no resolution at which the GRE is the right buy.

8 GB is a 1080p-only compromise, and only on the Nvidia side. A 5060 8 GB at €319 saves €243 over a 5060 Ti 16 GB and the penalty at 1080p is the mildest it gets (+29%, against +59% at 1440p). On AMD it is a trap: the 8 GB 9060 XT costs €341 against €414 for the 16 GB, and that €73 buys +66% at 1080p. Cheapest way into V-Cache at all is a 5500X3D, about €150 — AliExpress only, and unbenchmarked, so it gets no row; a 5600X3D reads 61 and it clocks lower.

The two columns are alternatives, not packages — mix them. A ✔ marks the better index per euro in that row, counting memory with the CPU because the socket drags the RAM cost along. Nothing stops an Intel chip driving a Radeon card, and at these prices that is repeatedly the cheaper build: the DDR4 Intel platforms win most of the CPU rows while Radeon wins nearly every GPU row. Read across, not down.

The 14600K on DDR4 is the sleeper. €229 plus a €240 DDR4 kit is a €469 platform against €619 for a 250K Plus on DDR5, for an index of 68 against 77 — so it wins wherever the GPU is the limit, which is why it takes 1440p and 4K mid but not 1080p, where the CPU is. Intel's ladder is flat here in any case: a 285K at €549 scores 78, two points over a €219 250K Plus, so the flagship appears nowhere on this card. AMD's ladder is not flat — a 9800X3D reaches 97.

1080p tops out deliberately low. The GPU field compresses to a 5.5× spread there and the CPU becomes the limit, so the 1080p high build pairs the best CPU with an RX 9070 / 5070 Ti rather than a 9070 XT or a 5080 — a flagship card at 1080p is money that buys almost nothing. On the AMD side mid and high even share the 9070 and differ by CPU alone, which is the clearest statement of the point on the card. At 4K the spread opens to 17× and the same money decides everything, which is why only that block reaches a 5090.

Measured: CPU index from seven Tom's Hardware, PCGH and ComputerBase test passes; GPU index per resolution from Tom's Hardware. Relative scales, not frame rates. Judgement: which CPU pairs with which resolution — CPU tests run at low resolution to isolate the chip, so there is only one CPU ladder. Not measured: RAM and storage. An outlined chip with a ° is derived rather than directly measured — either a GPU with no MSFS coverage, placed by interpolating TechPowerUp's aggregate between two cards we do measure, or an LGA1700 chip discounted onto DDR4 by the measured DDR5-vs-DDR4 factor (−6% on 12th gen, −10% on 14th).
